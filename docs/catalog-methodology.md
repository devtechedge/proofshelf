# Catalog methodology

## Purpose

ProofShelf teaches readers to separate a supplement marketing claim from the evidence that supports it. The catalog is a curated educational starting point, not a complete supplement encyclopedia or a systematic review of every product.

## Selection

The seed catalog contains exactly 100 ingredient families across nine categories:

| Category | Cards |
| --- | ---: |
| Vitamins and carotenoids | 14 |
| Minerals and trace minerals | 12 |
| Marine oils, essential fats, and carotenoids | 7 |
| Protein, amino acids, and performance | 15 |
| Cognition-related compounds | 3 |
| Botanicals and food-derived compounds | 21 |
| Medicinal mushrooms | 3 |
| Gut, fiber, and metabolic products | 13 |
| Joint, skin, sleep, and longevity specialties | 12 |

NOW Foods and iHerb public pages were used only to identify commonly marketed ingredient families. ProofShelf does not claim a live top-100 sales ranking, popularity score, endorsement, or efficacy inference from retailer pages. Product availability, price, reviews, and bestseller badges are not used as evidence.

## Source hierarchy

Evidence and safety context comes from high-trust institutional, review, trial, and regulatory sources, including:

- NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheets and updates.
- National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health evidence and safety summaries.
- MedlinePlus herbs and supplements monographs.
- Cochrane systematic reviews.
- PubMed Central and PubMed reviews or trials.
- FDA consumer, labeling, structure/function, and rulemaking information.

Every card stores source ids that resolve to the source registry. Retailer sources are marked `market-discovery`. A card must retain at least one non-retailer source. Where specific human evidence is absent, the card says so and uses a general institutional source rather than inventing a study.

## Claim contract

Each card contains:

- Stable id and slug.
- Ingredient name and aliases.
- Category, chemical form, and route.
- One concise, falsifiable common claim.
- One evidence verdict.
- A plain-language explanation and limitation.
- A concrete label-reading clue.
- A short safety note.
- Source ids and tags.
- A catalog review date of 26 November 2026.

Evidence does not transfer automatically between salts, extracts, routes, blends, or brands. For example, oral and topical GHK-Cu are not interchangeable, and fish, krill, cod-liver, and algal oils are separate products with different EPA/DHA profiles.

## Verdict definitions

### Well supported

A narrow claim with consistent, higher-quality human evidence. This does not mean the ingredient is right for every person or every product.

### Mixed evidence

Results vary by population, form, outcome, comparator, or study quality. The card explains the most important source of uncertainty.

### Early evidence

The claim relies on small, early, preclinical, biomarker-only, or otherwise limited human evidence. Early evidence is not presented as established benefit.

### Not supported

The claim is contradicted or lacks adequate evidence. This does not prove that every possible use of the ingredient is ineffective.

## Required nuances

- Collagen hydrolysate has promising but variable joint and skin evidence and is not equivalent to complete protein for muscle building.
- Triple-strength fish oil describes EPA/DHA concentration, not superior clinical outcomes.
- Zinc picolinate is not proven superior to other zinc forms.
- NAC has established medical uses in specific settings, which does not validate broad antioxidant or detox claims.
- Liposomal glutathione human evidence is early and does not establish anti-aging or disease-prevention benefits.
- GHK-Cu evidence is predominantly topical or preclinical, not robust oral evidence.
- Creatine monohydrate has strong support for resistance-training outcomes, not every athletic claim.
- High-dose beta-carotene increases lung-cancer risk in smokers.

## Safety and regulatory limits

ProofShelf rejects cure, treat, prevent-disease, guaranteed weight loss, guaranteed cognition, immune-boost, generic detox, and lifespan-extension claims. It does not provide personalized dosing or treatment advice.

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children, surgery, kidney or liver disease, autoimmune disease, transplant status, and anticoagulant use are reasons to involve a qualified clinician or pharmacist. U.S. supplements are not FDA-approved for safety and effectiveness before marketing. A structure/function disclaimer does not substantiate a claim. The 2026 NAC FDA action is a proposed rule, not a final rule.

## Maintenance

A catalog update should add or revise sources first, then update the affected claim, verdict, explanation, label clue, safety note, and review date. Tests must continue to prove 100 unique ids and slugs, correct category counts, resolvable source ids, required examples, and no em dash characters in public content.
